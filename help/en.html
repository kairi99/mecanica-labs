Help guide: MRUV with the DigiCart

A companion to the virtual lab: the theory of uniformly accelerated rectilinear motion (MRUV), how to use each instrument, how the uncertainties are computed, and what makes a real incline differ from a perfect MRUV. It does not replace the practice guide: it goes along with it.

1. MRUV theory

Straight-line motion is uniformly accelerated when the acceleration is constant. If the body starts at the origin with initial velocity v0 at t = 0, the equations are:

v(t) = v₀ + a·t
x(t) = x₀ + v₀·t + ½·a·t²
v² = v₀² + 2·a·(x − x₀)

The three characteristic graphs are a parabola for position, a sloped straight line for velocity and a horizontal line for acceleration:

Mean and instantaneous velocity

The mean velocity over an interval is the displacement divided by the time taken, v̄ = Δx/Δt. It is the slope of the chord joining two points of the x(t) graph. The instantaneous velocity is the limit of that chord as the interval shrinks: the slope of the tangent to x(t) at one instant. The shorter the interval, the closer the mean velocity gets to the instantaneous one at its midpoint.

Starting from rest

If v0 = 0 and x0 = 0, then x = ½·a·t² and v = a·t. Eliminating a between the two leaves a relation that does not contain it:

v_final = 2·x / t = 2·v̄

That is, the velocity at the end of the run is twice the mean velocity from rest. It is question 3 of the preparatory work, and task 9 checks it with your data.

Acceleration on an incline

For a frictionless sliding cart, a = g sin θ. If it rolls on wheels, part of the energy goes into spinning and the acceleration drops to g sin θ / (1 + k), where k is the ratio of the wheels' rotational inertia to the mass times the radius squared. Friction lowers it a bit more. So do not expect your a to equal g sin θ exactly.

2. How to use the lab

  1. Materials. In the inventory put every part on the table: rail, DigiCart, rack-and-pinion stand and its base, two-leg stand, receiver, screws, level, stopwatch, caliper and marks.
  2. Assembly. Put the rack stand on its base. Clamp one rail end in its clamp: that is the high end and the printed 0 of the scale must be there. At the opposite end go the two-leg stand and the receiver. Use all six screws (two per joint).
  3. Levelling. Put the level on the rail, leave the rack at zero and turn the foot of the two-leg stand until the bubble is centred. This removes the bench's own tilt before you create yours. If you raise the stand before levelling, the final incline is not the one you compute.
  4. 1.30° incline. Raise the high end with the rack. Measure the rail height at each stand with the caliper (below). The height difference must satisfy Δh = d·sin θ, with d the distance between the two stands measured along the rail. Work it out yourself: the checklist reminds you of the formula, not the answer. Fine-tune with the foot of the two-leg stand.
  5. Marks. Stick a mark every 10 cm from the highest point (A, 0 cm) up to at least 90 cm.
  6. Measuring. Put the front of the DigiCart on the 0 cm mark, choose the mark you will time to, release the cart and time it. Repeat 10 times per mark and copy each reading into Table 1.1.
  7. Analysis. The analysis panel computes the tasks of section 1.4 from your table and updates by itself. You answer the questions.

The DigiCart must never be off the rail: it rolls easily on any surface and can fall off the table.

The lab's checklist follows these steps and, with its "Show me" button, highlights the part on the stage and opens the panel you need. You can also replay the first-visit tour from that checklist.

3. The caliper

What it is for here

To measure the height of the rail above the table at each stand and check the incline. If the height difference is Δh and the stands are a distance d apart along the rail, then sin θ = Δh/d. For 1.30° you need Δh ≈ 0.0227·d: a few millimetres per decimetre.

The depth rod

A thin rod comes out of the back of the caliper body and moves with the slider. Rest the end of the body on the table and lower the rod until it touches the underside of the rail: the reading is the height. It is easier than opening the jaws around a tall stand.

Reading a 0.05 mm vernier

The main scale is in millimetres. The vernier has 20 divisions spanning 19 mm, so each is 0.95 mm long, that is 0.05 mm shorter than a main division. You read it in two steps:

  1. Read on the main scale the whole millimetres to the left of the vernier's zero.
  2. Find the vernier division that lines up with a main-scale mark. Its number, times 0.05 mm, gives the tenths and hundredths.

The digital caliper

It shows the measurement directly, with 0.01 mm resolution. Before measuring, close the jaws and press the zero button: a digital caliper can have a small zero error. If you do not zero it, that error is added to every reading.

4. The stopwatch and reaction time

You operate the stopwatch yourself: the exact moment of your press (key or tap) is what counts. Between the cart passing the mark and your finger reacting a fraction of a second goes by, and the same at the start. What the display shows is the true time plus the difference between the two delays.

Random and systematic error

  • Random: sometimes you react early and sometimes late. The readings spread around a value. Repeating and averaging reduces it: the uncertainty of the mean falls as 1/√n.
  • Systematic: if on average you stop later than you start, every reading is shifted the same way. Averaging does not fix it, because it is common to all of them.

Why 10 repetitions

With a single reading you do not know how much the quantity varies. With ten you can already estimate the standard deviation s and the uncertainty of the mean s/√10. More repetitions help little: they reduce the random error slowly and do not touch the systematic one.

The analysis panel includes a check against the DigiCart's log. There you see the mean offset of your readings from the true times: a similar offset at every mark is your reaction bias.

5. Uncertainty

Type A and Type B

Type A uncertainty comes from the scatter of repeated readings: for n readings with standard deviation s, uA = s/√n. Type B does not come from repeating but from what you know about the instrument: its resolution, its calibration or a reasonable bound on the error.

Conventions used in the lab

  • Digital instrument (stopwatch, digital caliper): the reading is known only down to the last digit r. A rectangular distribution with half-width r/2 is used, and u = (r/2)/√3 = r/√12 ≈ 0.29·r.
  • Analog scale (ruler, rail in millimetres, vernier): you read to the nearest division d, so the half-width is d/2 and u = d/√12. For a vernier, d is its least count (0.05 mm). Numerically it equals the digital case; the reasoning is what changes.
  • Careful: the "maximum error" d/2 that is sometimes taught is the half-width, not the standard uncertainty.
  • Reaction: the mean bias between start and stop is unknown. It is modelled as a rectangular distribution of ±0.1 s (an assumption of the virtual lab), with u = 0.1 s/√3 ≈ 0.058 s. It does not shrink when averaging.

Combining

Independent contributions are combined in quadrature: u = √(uA² + ures² + ureact²). For a computed quantity such as the mean velocity v̄ = Δx/Δt, the uncertainty is propagated: σv = √(σx1² + σx2² + v²(σt1² + σt2²))/Δt. Consecutive intervals share a mark, so their velocities are correlated.

Significant figures

Round the uncertainty to one or two significant figures (two if the first is 1 or 2) and the value to the same decimal place. Write 1.02 ± 0.06 s, not 1.0213 ± 0.058 s. Keep trailing zeros: 0.030 and 0.03 do not say the same thing.

Fitting x(t)

Since the times are the noisy quantity, the quadratic fit x = c₀ + c₁t + c₂t² weights each point with an effective uncertainty σeff² = σx² + (dx/dt)²·σt² (the effective-variance method). The acceleration is a = 2c₂. A reduced χ² near 1 means the error bars describe the scatter well.

6. Sources of error on an incline

This section answers, with the lab's model, question 4 of the preparatory work: why motion on an incline is not a perfect MRUV.

  • Friction and resistances: rolling resistance and bearing friction slow the cart. They lower the acceleration a little. Air also drags, more at higher speed.
  • Rotational inertia of the wheels: part of the potential energy goes into spinning the wheels. The acceleration is g sin θ/(1 + k), lower than g sin θ.
  • The release: if you push the cart as you let go, or place its front a little ahead, it starts with a non-zero velocity or position. The times come out shorter.
  • Bench and rail level: if the bench is not level, its tilt adds to yours. A bent or badly supported rail changes the slope along the run.
  • The angle: at 1.30°, an error of 0.1° in the angle is nearly 8 % in a, because a is proportional to sin θ.
  • Timing: reaction, stopwatch resolution and picking the wrong mark.
